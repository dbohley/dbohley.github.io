Integrated Respiratory Sensing and Gated Control for Autonomous Needle Insertion via CT Bore Robotic Arm

Emma Huang*, Moira Bohley*, Krishna Jamakhandi, Rebecca Fellbaum, Changwei Chen, Derek Chen, Ananya Rao, Sean Tutton, Albert Hsiao, Elizabeth Peiros, Michael C. Yip

* Equal contribution

Read the paper (arXiv)

Overview

CT-guided lung biopsy is still largely manual: the patient holds their breath while the clinician adjusts the needle by hand and repeats CT scans to confirm placement. We built an integrated sensing and control system that inserts the needle inside the CT bore in sync with the patient's breathing, with no breath hold. It tracks and predicts the breathing cycle, advances the needle only during exhalation, and stops when it detects abnormal breathing. The goal is shorter procedures, less patient discomfort, and less radiation exposure.

On a phantom test bench, all five end-to-end trials reached the full 40 mm depth without breaking the breathing-gating rules, and abrupt changes in breathing were detected in about 0.2 s.

My contributions

I designed the control system and the procedure state machine, and I implemented and ran the test-bench experiments that validate them.

  • Gated, delay-compensating control. The breathing signal reaches the controller roughly 300 ms late, so a controller that reacts to the latest reading will always be behind. I designed the controller to aim the needle at where the chest will be when it arrives, not where the sensor last saw it.
    • EKF breathing forecast. An Extended Kalman Filter fits a harmonic model of breathing to the contact-sensor signal and forecasts chest position one horizon ahead, where the horizon adds up sensing lag, computation time, needle-drive lag, and needle travel time. The forecast cut position error by 29.8% compared with the raw sensor.
    • Needle-drive compensation. I designed a lead compensator for the needle axis, chosen to minimize tracking lag at breathing rates while meeting phase-margin and noise limits.
    • Abnormal-breathing detection. The filter's innovation statistics flag when breathing departs from the model, catching abrupt changes in about 0.2 s without stopping for normal, gradual variation.
  • Procedure state machine. I designed the state machine that runs the insertion in four stages: approaching and contacting the torso, sensing breathing until the forecast is reliable enough to proceed, puncturing the skin during exhalation, and advancing the needle in increments on each exhalation until it reaches the target depth. It also defines a safe response to abnormal breathing in every stage.
  • Insertion planning. I developed algorithms that choose needle entry points and orientations to reach the target while avoiding bone collision and excessive tissue damage.
  • Test-bench validation. I implemented and ran the experiments that validate the system on a test bench, covering torso approach, breathing forecasting, the full insertion sequence, and anomaly detection.
  • Leading the paper. As co-first author, I led the project to ICRA submission, coordinating a 7-person team across the mechanical and software work.

Bi-manual Stabilization of the Cervical Spine for Safe Physical Human-Robot Interaction in Rolling Maneuvers

Elizabeth Peiros, Moira Bohley, Lucas Yager, Xiao Liang, Derek Chen, Rebecca Fellbaum, Chris D'Ambrosia, Ananya Rao, Michael C. Yip

Read the paper (arXiv)

Overview

Rolling an injured person onto their side, for example during casualty extraction, requires someone to hold the head and neck in line with the spine so a possible spinal injury isn't made worse. We adapted a Unitree G1 humanoid with compliant grip pads and developed a bimanual algorithm that holds the head as firmly as safety limits allow while controlling neck rotation through the roll.

With motion prediction, the robot held axial (left-right) neck alignment within 0.21° of novice human responders.

My contributions

  • Real-time bimanual inverse kinematics. I built the optimization-based IK that coordinates both 7-DoF arms (14 joints) at every control step, weighing hand position, hand orientation, and smoothness against the previous step so the head stays aligned with the spine as the torso rolls. I developed it first in a digital twin of the setup before moving to hardware.
  • Head–torso pose tracking. I built the vision pipeline that measures cervical alignment error: ArUco markers on the head and torso, tracked by the robot's head camera and mapped into clinical axes (axial rotation, flexion/extension, lateral bending).
  • Bimanual grasp control. I developed the grasp controller, which estimates contact force from joint-torque feedback and squeezes until it reaches a calibrated grip threshold.
  • Hardware integration and sim-to-real on the G1. I brought the tracking, IK, and grasp control together on the Unitree G1 humanoid for the real-world experiments, and worked through the problems that only showed up on hardware:
    • Vision robustness. In uneven lighting, the camera skipped frames and the ArUco pose estimates would sometimes flip orientation. I added filtering that rejects flipped and outlier poses, holds through skipped frames, and rate-limits the error signal, so bad detections never turned into sudden arm motion.
    • Head weight and friction. On the real setup, the head's weight and the friction in the neck joint changed how the arms held and rotated it, so I accounted for both in the control to keep the grip secure and the rotation smooth.
