Integrated Respiratory Sensing and Gated Control for Autonomous Needle Insertion via CT Bore Robotic Arm

Emma Huang*, Moira Bohley*, Krishna Jamakhandi, Rebecca Fellbaum, Changwei Chen, Derek Chen, Ananya Rao, Sean Tutton, Albert Hsiao, Elizabeth Peiros, Michael C. Yip

* Equal contribution

Read the paper (arXiv)

Overview

CT-guided needle procedures such as biopsies and ablations need the needle to reach a target that moves every time the patient breathes. We put a robotic arm inside the CT bore, sense the patient's breathing in real time, and gate the insertion so the needle advances only during a safe, repeatable window of the breathing cycle. [Add the headline result, e.g. targeting error in mm.]

My contributions

I designed and built the control side of the system: the gated insertion controller (Methods, Section D) and the state machine that runs the procedure (Methods, Section E), and I took part in the experiments that validate them.

  • Gated, delay-compensating control (Section D). I developed the controller that times needle insertion within the breathing cycle. Both the respiratory signal and the arm's actuation arrive with delay, so the controller compensates for that latency and the needle advances during the safe window rather than after it has closed. [One sentence on how the compensation works.]
  • Procedure state machine (Section E). I designed the state machine that sequences the autonomous insertion and decides when the robot is allowed to move. [Name the main states and what triggers each transition.]
  • Respiratory motion estimation. I used tactile sensing in an Extended Kalman Filter to estimate chest motion for accurate needle insertion.
  • Insertion planning. I developed algorithms that choose needle entry points and orientations to reach the target while avoiding bone collision and excessive tissue damage.
  • Simulation and experiments. I implemented simulation and physical testing of the arm in ROS 2, and took part in the experiments evaluating the controller and state machine. [Which experiments, and the result.]
  • Leading the paper. As co-first author, I led the project to ICRA submission, coordinating a 7-person team across the mechanical and software work.

Bi-manual Stabilization of the Cervical Spine for Safe Physical Human-Robot Interaction in Rolling Maneuvers

Elizabeth Peiros, Moira Bohley, Lucas Yager, Xiao Liang, Derek Chen, Rebecca Fellbaum, Chris D'Ambrosia, Ananya Rao, Michael C. Yip

Read the paper (arXiv)

Overview

When an injured person has to be rolled, for example during casualty extraction, their head and neck must stay in line with the spine to avoid worsening a possible spinal injury. This usually takes trained responders. We developed a two-arm controller that stabilizes the cervical spine through the roll and demonstrated it on a Unitree G1 humanoid. [Add the headline result.]

My contributions

  • Digital twin and inverse kinematics. I created a digital twin of the bimanual setup and used nonlinear-optimization inverse kinematics to coordinate the two 7-DoF arms, solving jointly for position, orientation, and smoothness so the patient's head stays aligned with the spine during movement.
  • Head–torso pose tracking. I built a vision pipeline using ArUco markers to track head and torso pose, which is how we measure cervical spine alignment error.
  • Bimanual grasp control. I developed a grasp controller that estimates contact force from joint-torque feedback and uses it to regulate grip.
  • Hardware integration on the G1. I integrated the system onto the Unitree G1 humanoid platform for the real-world experiments.
